It is important to remember that this game is likely to be short on thumbnail material. Especially since most of the screen will be essentially black during most of the game. For this reason, I intend to place a fairly high amount of effort on getting the HUD and UI in general looking very good. That also goes for the in-game weapon and shield graphics. We deliberately intend to be over the top with these. This is not a simulator, and the rule of cool always trumps a realistic depiction.

While the rule of cool does trump a realistic depiction, I should also say that I have a strong preference for clarity, and avoiding clutter. There is a maxim that says "If it doesn't have a gameplay purpose, do not render it". I like this, although I'm not sure that it should always be followed rigorously, and it depends on how you define a "gameplay purpose". Immersion is a purpose, and almost everything could be dumped in that category. As always, a nuanced interpretation is far more useful than a dogmatic, literalist interpretation. At least when it comes to the English language. The relevance to this game's aesthetic design is that I do not intend to add anything to ship or outf models that does not have a defined purpose. Some items will be added purely for aesthetics, such as radiators. This game will not have a heat management system, so they do not have a "gameplay" purpose; nonetheless, their purpose is defined. Put another way: "Greebles considered harmful". Keeping the models fairly simple speeds their production, reduces incidence of apparent hit collision detection errors, and makes the ships more immediately recognizable. It also has more subtle benefits, such as allowing for annotated blueprint-style diagrams that will, for those interested in them, help give the player the impression of a detailed and coherent world. If the player is curious about something regarding a ship's physical design, the blueprint diagram will probably be able to shed some light on it. If the player notices this tight correlation between bits and bobs on the ship model and their "lore" purpose within the game world, they will spend time inspecting ship models more in the future. These diagrams will be entirely optional to interact with, and hidden behind unobtrusive buttons. Perhaps one in the shipyard listing for each ship, and another in the ship properties menu, where the player can view his ship's specifications, cargo, equipment, legal status, etc. These diagrams wouldn't be worth it if not for the fact that they can be generated with AI, and require very little processing in order to make them into game ready assets. 

Another similar idea is "Artist's impression" images, which I intend to make after having modeled the ship completely. These should cost very little time to make, and should spruce up things like shipyard listings, loading screens, and possibly some other areas. They would not be worth it if not for the fact that AI can do a very good job on such things, especially when you provide it with renders of the already extant ship model. These "Artist's impression" images are not intended to have serve a critical role - they are flavor.

Needless to say, all of the modeling will be done manually, most likely by myself. I would like to have someone else do it, but I can't afford to pay someone. One of the costs of using my own software ecosystem, rather than something like Unreal, is that collaborators have a high onboarding burden. As of the start of this project (start of Q4 '26), no AI comes close to being able to create the kind of game ready assets I have in mind. When I say "models", I'm referring to the base mesh, the proprietary material system I'll be building, the collider set (locational damage means that a ship requires an engine collider, a hull collider, etc.), the meshes for any separable or animating equipment such as turrets or gimbaling engines, the shield collider, any shield rendering mesh, the sockets for weapon exit points, engine exhaust plume exit points, running lights, and probably more.

When I grew up, fax machines were commonplace, even though they were decidedly old and deprecated by that time.
